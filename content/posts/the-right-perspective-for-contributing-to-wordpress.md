## Who is "they"?

I've been using WordPress for 8 years, and developing custom themes and plugins for 3. In that time I've come to greatly appreciate the versatility and growth of the platform, which is not only being updated but evolving. When I deliver a WordPress-based project to a client, we talk about how to make changes to their site and the importance of keeping their site up to date. I found myself referring to an ambiguous "they" in these discussions; i.e. "_they_ release frequent security patches" or "_they_ are encouraging the best practice of X here." But who is the they? I've known for years that it was an open source community of volunteers, but it wasn't until recently that I grew determined to overcome the hurdles and actually contribute.

## The main hurdle is self-inflicted

The hurdles are more process and social oriented than technical. In fact you can contribute to WordPress without writing a single line of code. But in spite of the extensive contributing handbook and guides, learning the ropes still takes time. It's easy to make a faux pas by, say, making a joke in the wrong Slack channel (which I've \*cough\* never done). The first time I tried to contribute was by suggesting a fix for a very old (8+ years) annoyance that the _current_ version number is not shown when WordPress suggests an update to X.XX version. I thought this would be a simple, good first bug. But the process went like this:

1.  Research issue
2.  Create a ticket
3.  Find out a ticket already existed and that mine was being merged into it
4.  See past potential solutions and that the issue is quite old
5.  Write a proposed code change
6.  Attend a meeting on Slack and try to get some attention for the issue
7.  Be told it would be reviewed but that unless it captured the interest of one of the core maintainers it wouldnt be touched.
8.  Try to get it more attention, but grow frustrated that it didn't seem important to others

Those last items, which were told directly to me, were honest but discouraging. What was the point of trying to help if my work would sit on the shelf growing dusty? It led me to stop trying to contribute for a year, and I do still see it as a entry barrier. But while it's frustrating from the perspective of, "how can I get this particular issue fixed," it makes sense from the perspective of the WordPress project as a whole. What gets changed **should** be partially determined by a stable group of dedicated people. If you can't make a good and broad enough case that what you want to see changed should get changed in the way you want, then it's not worth it for "all of WordPress" to change in that way.

## The better perspective

Instead of, "how can I get the WordPress community to change X," I'd suggest the stereotypical question reversal: "what does the WordPress contributing team need from me?" In other words, what work that _already has the attention and support of the decision makers_ can I help with? Contributing to WordPress isn't about getting your pet issue fixed. It's about helping make the software you use better for everyone. When you view it that way, the major hurdle of trying to wedge yourself into the mix disappears. You're filling a gap, and there's no need to push yourself in. You're needed. The new steps, nearly a year after I first attempted to "contribute," were far more successful.

1.  Check out the tickets marked for the next release, and choose one that still needed testing
2.  Research the issue and try to replicate and/or solve it
3.  Post my results on the issue

That's it. Contributed. And since my results were enough to lead to discovering a new but related bug, I had my _next_ way to contribute. I created a new ticket for it, did more research, and kept following the trail. Since the new bug I discovered was related to a feature being rolled out, it got attention on its own merits. I didn't have to try to fight for attention. Instead, I watched the ticket for any discussion, asked for feedback, and got assistance in helping steer it through the development cycle. After two weeks, I had written a fix (actually 2) for the bug as well as a unit test (again, 2 versions), and the change had been discussed, approved, and committed. It felt like a real contribution. It was. I'm still learning some of the best ways to "grease the wheels", but I'm not trying to steer the cart anymore. Already I've made connections, and more importantly, been able to make a _real_ contribution by applying my skills of research, debugging and troubleshooting potential solutions. It wasn't my 'pet issue' that got fixed, but it was a real one, and it was one that I was able to resolve for every user of WordPress everywhere. Each of those millions of sites will have 4 lines of my code. That feels _awesome._
