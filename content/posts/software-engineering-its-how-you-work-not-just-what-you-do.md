Anyone who has interviewed and hired enough software engineers can tell you that technical skill is only one piece of the puzzle.

For instance, I love coding challenges. (In fact, I am currently participating in the [Advent of Code 2020](https://adventofcode.com/2020)). But your ability to quickly write an algorithm does _not_ mean you can write software.

Other skills--like how you talk to people, manage your time, and follow through--are at least as important as your technical chops. And unlike learning a new programming language, they cannot be so easily taught.

However, you _can_ work on them. Below I share a few of the most important traits that I've found make a huge difference in an engineer's success, and some questions to think about for each of them.

## Humility

Humility doesn't just make you more pleasant to work with. It also affects the quality of your work.

If you actually think your work is always perfect, then you won't be open to improving it. And if your work from 3 months ago was also "perfect", then **_you_** haven't improved.

But perhaps thinking you're "the best" just means "better than everyone else" on the team. This, too, is toxic. You may get away with it on smaller teams and projects, but sooner or later the thinking will come back to bite you. Because, as a human, you **_will_** make mistakes.

I have seen people like this bend over backwards to explain why their mistakes weren't actually mistakes at all. They end up blaming their teammates, former employees, technology, clients--anything they can think of. This creates a judgmental, divisive, and highly negative culture for everyone.

Instead, recognize that you are continuously learning. Remember that even the best software in the world created by huge teams of experienced engineers has bugs. And take my word that the best and most talented software engineers I have known have been the ones quickest to admit they make mistakes.

A culture of humility is the strongest antidote to imposter syndrome. You're not a fraud just because you're not perfect. See? None of us are.

## Initiative

At age 16, in my very first job as a theme park employee, I learned that I hate being micro-managed.

Now that I manage others, just about the very last thing I want to do is micro-manage. That's mostly because it's demeaning and I hate it myself, but also because I don't have the time.

(Note: it is _absolutely_ my job to step in when needed. There should be no task a manager is "above" doing when needed.)

Too many developers view their tasks as a list of "inputs". They'll follow the steps, complete the tasks given, and generally do them well. But if something is _missing_, or if some of the "input" is "invalid", the train screeches to a halt.

But _some_ are able to go a step further, zoom out, and ask, "What needs to be done here?"

Employees with initiative are ones to whom I can give a project and **trust** that they will do whatever is needed to get it done well. There may be missing information--they will figure out who to ask. Or perhaps there are vague, conflicting requirements from the client--they will work to refine them. Maybe they don't have experience with a key technology--they'll learn it.

Even projects that have stalled out in the hands of others begin moving again in their hands. And clients feel less like they're working with a call-center representative, and more like they're working with an experienced, devoted consultant.

## Synthesis

A great engineer can synthesize complex parts into a cohesive whole.

This is hugely important for planning software architecture. Even a modest website relies on multiple layers of hardware, software, APIs, languages and network requests. It is relatively easy to make each layer "work". It is much harder to understand how they fit together.

This has grown even more important with the rise of cloud infrastructure. Cloud providers like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft ofter literally hundreds of services that can be interconnected to power an application. Having a _clear vision_ about the whole allows you to plan robust, scalable, and secure systems. This "vision" is what software architecture diagrams attempt to elicit.

Even with existing software, synthesis is a big part of proper debugging. It is often important to step back from an immediate problem and contextualize it in its place in the broader system to determine the true cause of an issue.

But synthesis goes beyond technical vision. How does this project fit into our business objectives? How will this feature co-exist with the existing ones? What _exactly_ is our client trying to accomplish with this new product? Even a single meeting must be synthesized properly if you want to avoid "missing the forest for the trees".

## And Many More

There are of course _many_ other traits that matter a lot. Communication, respect, a sense of team, drive, innovation, endurance...the list keeps going.

If there is a single lesson here, it is that _how you work_ is just as important as _what you do_. Software engineering is much more than writing code. It's about _what_ code to write, how to create software _well_, and how what you're doing fits into the bigger picture. That picture includes colleagues, legacy software, clients, business requirements, and so much more. Having technical skills alone just doesn't cut it.

While demeanor, natural talents, and personality play into these traits, they are mostly about attitude. We can reflect on how we view them, and, if necessary, how our view needs to shift.

**Humility**

*   Do I secretly think I'm better than everyone else on my team?
*   When others disagree with me, do I hear them out before assuming they're wrong?
*   When someone points out a mistake in my work, do I immediately defend it?
*   When someone else shines, do I celebrate their accomplishments?
*   When I don't know something, am I open to others about it?

**Initiative**

*   Do I take pride in my projects?
*   Do I skip over things I know "should" be done, because I haven't been asked to do them?
*   Do I step back to ask, "what needs to be done here?"
*   Have I considered the quality I would want if I were using the software?

**Synthesis**

*   Do I dive in and start working on a task without caring to understand how it fits in to the broader project?
*   Do I think about how my work fits into the work of my teammates?
*   Do I listen to coworkers, clients, and others for what their underlying concerns are?
*   Do I keep the 'big picture' in mind when I am working on a small piece of it?
