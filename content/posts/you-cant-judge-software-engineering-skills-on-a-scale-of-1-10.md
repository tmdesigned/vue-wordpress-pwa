Okay, you "can". But you shouldn't ask job applicants to rate themselves on a scale of 1-10 in various languages, skills, and toolsets. It's ambiguous, subjective, and unfair. There's a better scale anyways: the competency scale.

In addition to evaluating candidates, a competency scale can also serve as an excellent career development planning tool. We'll go over both use-cases below, right after we look at the problems with the traditional 1-10 scale.

### The Candidate's Conundrum

How often have you seen this kind of question on an interview assessment, or heard it asked in an interview?

![](/img/uploads/2021/02/scale-example-1024x287.png)

I'm guilty of asking this of candidates, though I have the ulterior motive of assessing their _humility_. ("You're a 9 in JavaScript? That's fantastic. Let's talk prototypal inheritance.") Humility, [as I've written before](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/software-engineering-its-how-you-work-just-what-do-taylor-morgan/?trackingId=2arHHM03F3RGvH%2FD61YdfQ%3D%3D), is one of the key skills of a successful software engineer.

But if you've been asked this, you know the problem you face. Rate yourself too high, and they'll think you're arrogant or lying. Rate yourself too low, and you won't seem like you have what it takes for the job.

How can a broad, complex skill be reduced to a number anyways? I always want to ask, "What aggregation method am I supposed to use here?" Are you supposed to average out all the different elements that make up a skill, or go by the part you're best at?

In my experience, _most_ people with solid ability in the skill will say 7 or 8. This tells the interviewer precious little.

Aside from the unfair guessing-game it places candidates in, it's entirely subjective. How is a 6 different from an 8? Am I a 7 if I can do it well, but still have to Google things (like most candidates). Whatever the answer is, do you expect the scale to stay consistent between candidates? Not likely!

Luckily, there's a _better_ scale. A scale that is founded on solid psychological principles.

### Hierarchy of Competence

The "[Hierarchy of Competence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_stages_of_competence)" suggests there are 4 phases in learning a skill, and can be pictured as a pyramid:

![](/img/uploads/2021/02/hierarchy-of-competence-pyramid-1024x534.png)

From bottom to top, these levels represent our journey as we discover and master a skill:

1.  **Unconscious incompetence:** I'm not skilled at this, and I don't even realize how unskilled I am, or why it's worth learning to do it better.
2.  **Conscious incompetence:** I see that I'm unskilled, and the value in learning to do it better. I'll start trying, though I will make a lot of mistakes.
3.  **Conscious competence:** I am capable of doing this well, though it takes a good deal of effort on my part. I may have to slow down or retrace my steps at times.
4.  **Unconscious competence:** This comes naturally to me now. I can do this well, and can intuit the right way of doing it without having to think about it.

At first, we "don't know what we don't know". When we come to appreciate our inability, we can begin to improve. It takes great effort at first, and involves mistakes. But eventually, it becomes second-nature to us.

The final stage, "unconscious competence," does not mean that the skill takes no work at all. Some things will naturally remain complex. But we know what to do, and see naturally the best way to do it.

### Translating to Levels

Those competency levels are a bit of a mouthful, and we aren't interviewing candidates to give them a psychology lesson. We can translate them into more user-friendly skill levels:

![](/img/uploads/2021/02/skill-levels-1024x255.png)

These levels ("Beginner," "Novice," "Practitioner," and "Expert") correspond to the stages above. The terms are familiar and friendly enough that most people can quickly grasp them.

More importantly, they correspond to how learning a software engineering skill (or any skill, really) actually works. We _don't_ learn discretely on a scale of 1-10. You will not hear software engineers saying casually, "I was a 7 yesterday, but then I read this book on web assembly, and now I'm an 8!".

Scales like that are oversimplifications, because life is not a role-playing game. The truth is that with software engineering in particular, you're continually learning and improving.

But you _do_ progress from knowing nothing about it ("Beginner"), to learning it a little but doing it poorly ("Novice"), to doing it competently, but with some effort, on a regular basis ("Practitioner"), to doing it well by second-nature ("Expert").

When I'm interviewing candidates, what I want to know is where they are on that journey for the different skills needed in the job.

By the way, even an Expert will still look things up. Googling things is a normal, healthy part of the job. (Frankly, I'd rather you look something up to make sure you're doing it right. Doctors do this, too.) But the _intuition_, the second-nature understanding of how a problem should be approached, can't be faked.

### TaylorScript, an Example of Learning a Skill

These levels are also easy to apply to a technical skill, and most software engineers will relate to them from their own experience. Imagine a software engineer hears of a fictitious new, possibly revolutionary language, TaylorScript.

At first, they have no idea what it is, why it matters, how it's different, or why they should bother learning it. They may hear it mentioned in the break-room, see it mentioned on a blog, or read it on a job description. They are a **Beginner**.

Eventually, they run into this word enough times that they decide to do some research. They might discover TaylorScript is semantically brilliant, works flawlessly across browsers, and handles heavy computation tasks with ease. Excited, they read the "Getting Started" guide, download the test environment and begin playing with some tutorials. They are a **Novice**.

At this point, they will have a lot of misconceptions and false assumptions about TaylorScript. They will make mistakes, and not be able to see the better way of doing things. But they are learning, and they _recognize_ that they are, in fact, making mistakes.

And as they iron out the wrinkles in their knowledge, they progress. They make fewer and fewer mistakes, though they have to think hard about how to do things the right way. They may have to rewrite sections of their code, and still make occasional mistakes. They are a **Practitioner**, able to do the work and do it well, though with a fair bit of effort.

After a great deal of experience, they do not have to struggle so much to use TaylorScript. They have a broad and deep knowledge of how it works, and why it's set up the way it is. When reviewing others' code, they see hidden anti-patterns and mistakes without even trying. They can predict what works fine now but will be a problem later. They are an **Expert.**

Since the scale relates to how we actually learn in real life, it's easy for candidates to apply to their own skills. They may not be able to tell the difference between a "6" or a "7", but they will be able to tell you whether they are a Novice or a Practitioner.

### Candidate Assessments

Even with friendlier names, the levels still need some explanation when evaluating candidates. A short key can help keep everyone on the same page. For instance:

1.  BEGINNER - I haven't done this very much yet, or at all.
2.  NOVICE - I've started learning this, and can get by (slowly). I can't help others much except as a fellow learner.
3.  PRACTITIONER - I'm familiar with this and can do it well, but I have to concentrate to do it right. I can help others, but may have to look it up or try a few things.
4.  EXPERT - I'm very experienced with this, and it comes naturally to me now. I can definitely help others who have questions about this.

I like framing the descriptions with the team-oriented aspect of, "how much can I help others with this?" This reinforces a culture of collaboration, and helps candidates think about their skills from a different angle.

Their answers will be specific enough to tell you what you need to know, without getting bogged down in tiny differences.

Is it a skill central to the job? You're looking for at least a practitioner. A senior role that will be mentoring others in this skill? That's an expert. A tangential skill they can learn on the job? A novice in that is fine.

### Career Development

I originally developed this model to help my employees identify areas they want to focus their professional development on. At Losant, each employee has a dedicated professional development fund and an expectation that half a day each week can be spent on side projects and learning. This year we started an annual career development planning process, and I wanted something to help the software engineers on my team think about their skills.

When used in this case, it's important to make it clear that this is a career tool, not a performance evaluation.

Drawing from resources like the [Developer Roadmaps](http://roadmap.sh/), I put together a spreadsheet of skills used in their role. I added one column for them to rate themselves in each skill, and another with a "target" rating. For fun, it creates a visual graph showing potential areas they could work on.

### Competency Scale Summary

This kind of scale is the beginning of the conversation, not the end of it. You won't _really_ know how a candidate's skills stack up until you've worked with them for several months.

The 1-10 scales are pretending to be more accurate than they really are! But the competency scale is honest in its breakdown, reflects how people actually progress, and tells you what you really need to know.

Precisely because it so closely relates to actual learning, it's also useful as a career development tool. An engineer can recognize where they are a novice or a beginner, and choose to work on those areas. They can feel confident that even though they haven't mastered every element of a skill or language, they can still be a solid practitioner of it. And when they call themselves an Expert at something, they know it's not presumption. They've simply honed their skill to the point they naturally do it well. That's a measurable, achievable, and worthy goal.
